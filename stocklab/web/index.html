A MODEL YOU CAN EXPLAIN

Look under the hood.

Start with intuition. Then follow the mathematics.

01 / MONTE CARLO

Rehearse possible futures.

Generate many plausible demand and supply outcomes, calculate profit in each, and summarize the distribution.

Imagine rehearsing a shop’s opening day thousands of times. Each rehearsal has different customers and deliveries. The pattern across rehearsals helps you prepare; none predicts the exact day.
02 / SELECTION & EVALUATION

Keep the exam unseen.

Choose a policy on one set of simulated worlds. Freeze that choice and measure its results on independent worlds.

Practising the same exam can make you look better than you are. A fresh exam tests whether the choice still works. A fresh simulation only tests your choice within the assumed model.
03 / TAIL RISK

The bad days matter.

The worst 5% average profit averages the lowest 5% of profits. It is not the fifth percentile, which is a boundary rather than an average.

Two bridges may carry the same average traffic. You still want to know how each behaves during the worst storms. Average profit is ordinary traffic; the lower tail describes those difficult days.
04 / COMMON RANDOM NUMBERS

Compare on the same weather.

Both policies face identical demand and delivery outcomes within each replicate. The confidence interval uses paired profit differences.

Test two umbrellas in the same rainstorm. Testing one in drizzle and one in a downpour confuses the umbrella with the weather. Shared random inputs make the comparison fairer.
05 / SCRAMBLED SOBOL

Cover the space evenly.

Sobol points deliberately cover the input space. Independent scrambles support error estimates; individual points within a scramble are not independent.

Inspect a field by spreading your checks across it instead of dropping every check wherever a coin lands. Scrambling moves the inspection pattern. Repeating independently reveals how variable the estimate is.
06 / MODEL LIMITS

Precision is not truth.

More paths reduce numerical error. They cannot repair incorrect demand assumptions, missing seasonal effects, or unrealistic supply contracts.

A sharper camera gives a clearer picture of whatever it faces. If it faces the wrong street, extra sharpness won’t show your destination. More simulations sharpen the model you chose.

The accounting identity

Profit = selling price × units sold + salvage × leftovers − unit cost × ordered units − penalty × unmet demand

Demand follows a lognormal distribution. A Gaussian copula can couple high demand with supply disruption. All values describe one period, one item, and continuous quantities. The repository study guide develops each assumption, formula, algorithm, and extension.
